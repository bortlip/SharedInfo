01
A closer look

The 1990 argument

Searle separates three claims: the mind is a program, the brain is a digital computer, and brain operations can be computationally simulated. He argues that these are different questions and targets the idea that computation is an intrinsic physical property. Searle, Is the Brain a Digital Computer? ↗

02
A closer look

Multiple implementations

Any mapping from physical states to formal symbols requires a choice of description. Searle contends that describing a physical system as running a particular computation therefore depends on interpreters, whereas the system's physical causal powers do not. Searle 1990 ↗

03
A closer look

The computationalist response

A serious implementation criterion can demand more than an arbitrary mapping from events to labels: it can require causal relations that would hold under alternative inputs and other counterfactual conditions. This is the motivation behind later work on implementation and causal organization. SEP, Mind and Body ↗

04
A closer look

What it does and does not show

Even if computational descriptions are partly conventional, the computer's physical state transitions are real. The disputed step is whether a system's computational description captures the particular objective properties relevant to semantic understanding. That needs a criterion of implementation and a theory of content.

Historical note

This is a later argument and a set of later criticisms, not the continuation of a direct 1980 exchange. It should be read as a deeper branch, not as something Searle said to Wilensky or Fodor in the original commentary sequence.
