THE QUESTION TO SETTLE

Searle's deepest later challenge asks whether computation is something a physical system does intrinsically or a description an observer can impose.

This chapter analyzes the relevant primary works and marks the boundary between direct historical exchanges, later independent arguments, and the evaluation of their premises.

ONE SIDE

A mapping from physics to formal symbols can depend on an interpreter.

↔
THE CHALLENGE

A system's counterfactual-supporting causal transitions can be objectively constrained.

A LATER ARGUMENT, NOT A DIRECT 1980 EXCHANGE

Searle separates three questions that are too easily conflated.

In 'Is the Brain a Digital Computer?' (1990), Searle distinguishes whether the mind is a computer program, whether the brain is a digital computer, and whether brain processes can be simulated computationally.

He answers the simulation question affirmatively and the idea that the mind is just a program negatively. He spends the paper examining the third issue: what would it mean to discover that the brain is intrinsically a computer, rather than merely admitting some computational description?

This is an important historical boundary. The essay is a later, independent argument, not an answer he gave directly to Wilensky, Fodor or the Systems Reply in 1980.

THE OBSERVER-RELATIVITY ARGUMENT

Symbols and computational states depend on an interpretation.

Searle notes that a binary 1 or 0 could be realized in voltage levels, switches, hydraulics, or biological activity. The physical process does not carry a uniquely privileged label '1' or '0'; a computational description maps physical states onto formal states.

He argues that under a permissive mapping, many ordinary physical objects can be described as implementing arbitrary computations. If the mappings are too unconstrained, 'is a digital computer' ceases to mark an objective physical kind. In his conclusion, he denies that the brain could be discovered to be intrinsically digital in the ordinary empirical sense.

That critique attacks the interpretation-independent status of the computational description. It does not deny that an actual electronic computer undergoes real physical state changes.

THE COUNTERFACTUAL RESPONSE

A useful implementation must work under alternative inputs, not merely fit one recorded trajectory.

A critic can distinguish arbitrary labeling from a real causal implementation. Suppose I map arbitrary states of a rock onto states in a finite automaton after observing a particular run. That may mimic the observed sequence, but it does not follow that if a different input arrived, the rock would make the corresponding transition.

The counterfactual objection demands a physically supported mapping: when different legal inputs occur, the machine reliably follows the stipulated state-transition relations. That gives a principled difference between a computer executing code and a random physical object being interpreted as one after the fact.

This distinction is developed in work by Jack Copeland and David Chalmers and surveyed in the Stanford Encyclopedia's account of physical computation. It is not a documented direct reply by Searle to a particular later sentence of this guide.

THE CONCESSION A COMPUTATIONALIST STILL NEEDS

Objective implementation alone would not automatically prove understanding.

Even if physical computation is objectively distinguishable from arbitrary interpretation, Searle can agree that computers genuinely execute algorithms while denying that execution constitutes understanding. The debate then returns to intrinsic semantics.

A strict computational theory therefore has two separate burdens: identify an objective implementation relation, and explain why a particular realized computation has the causal properties relevant to understanding. Confusing those burdens makes the argument appear settled when only one part has been addressed.

WHERE THIS LEAVES THE CHINESE ROOM

Causal organization is real; whether it is semantic is another question.

A physically implemented program causally affects hardware, and a computational characterization can explain many patterns in its behavior. Searle's point is that a formal description need not capture everything causally relevant to a cognitive phenomenon.

The computationalist reply is that a suitable description may track exactly the right causal organization. Which particular organizations instantiate intentionality remains the substantive issue. This is a deeper version of the original disagreement rather than a final defeat of either theory.
