# Product Vision

## Purpose

The boarding simulator should be a playful laboratory where people can race boarding methods, create absurd-but-recognizable passenger situations, and understand why one flight boards smoothly while another turns into a tiny rolling civilization crisis.

Scientific fairness still matters: methods should face matched passengers, matched random traits, and matched disruptions. But the primary experience should be entertaining and visually legible rather than austere.

## Product pillars

### 1. A race worth watching

The six methods should feel like competitors. The user should be able to see momentum, bottlenecks, comebacks, and decisive moments rather than merely wait for six clocks to stop.

### 2. People, not dots

Passengers should gradually acquire recognizable traits, icons, labels, reactions, and stories. A user should be able to understand that a particular passenger is a child, an experienced traveler, a slow bag stower, a restroom returner, or a member of a specific party.

### 3. Controlled chaos

Real boarding is memorable because of exceptions. The simulator should support optional disruptive events and colorful archetypes while applying the same event script fairly across methods.

### 4. Explain the outcome

After a run, the simulator should identify the moments and mechanisms that mattered: seat conflicts, bag delays, long queues, family coordination, event disruptions, and useful parallel work.

### 5. Easy experimentation

Presets, shareable URLs, replay, and approachable controls should make it effortless to try "holiday flight," "business route," "perfect compliance," or "maximum chaos."

## Tone

The simulator may be funny and affectionate about familiar travel behavior. It should avoid cruelty, punching down, or presenting disability, children, age, body type, intoxication, or anxiety as inherently comic. Humor should come from situations and systems colliding.

## Non-goals

- Predicting the exact boarding time of a specific real flight
- Hiding uncertain assumptions behind an "accurate" label
- Adding realism that cannot be seen, explained, or explored
- Turning every person into a complicated autonomous agent before the UI can communicate what is happening
- Sacrificing determinism and fair comparison for random spectacle

## Success

A successful version makes users say both:

> "That was fun to watch."

and

> "Oh, now I understand why that method won."
