Should the city build the tunnel?

What is happening?

Nobody can improve by switching.

At 40 drivers, both choices take 45 minutes. A commuter who treats their own effect on the whole system as negligible has no reason to change modes. This is the mode-choice Nash equilibrium.

Driving25 + 0.50 × 40 = 45Transit35 + 0.25 × 40 = 45

The intersection is the equilibrium: if one mode is faster, commuters move toward it. Opening the tunnel shifts the driving curve down by five minutes, but it does not freeze behavior at the old mode split.

The paradox

Before45 min40 drive · 60 transit
→
After adaptation50 min60 drive · 40 transit

The tunnel really is five minutes faster. That is precisely the problem. It attracts transit riders to the road. More cars rebuild congestion; fewer transit riders make transit less attractive. Commuters keep responding individually until both modes meet again — at a worse equilibrium.

Individual incentive: take whichever mode is faster for you.
Collective outcome: everybody can end up five minutes worse off.
Model assumptions and the tiny bit of math

This is a deliberately transparent teaching model, not a calibrated transportation forecast. Let D be the number of drivers out of 100 commuters. Before the tunnel, driving takes 25 + 0.50D minutes. The tunnel removes the five-minute mountain detour, so afterward driving takes 20 + 0.50D. Transit takes 35 + 0.25D: as riders leave transit for cars, service becomes less frequent or convenient.

The equilibrium shown here is the standard nonatomic/Wardrop form of a Nash equilibrium. We draw 100 commuter dots because humans enjoy dots more than measure theory, but the model treats one commuter as too small to change the cost functions by themselves. Equating the two used-mode costs gives D = 40 before the tunnel and D = 60 afterward.

The key assumption behind the Downs-Thomson mechanism is the ridership feedback in transit. If transit service did not weaken as riders left, this particular paradox would disappear from this toy model.
