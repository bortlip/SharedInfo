The question

Does the tunnel improve the equilibrium, or merely improve the road?

The paradox has not happened yet. Establish a stable baseline, build the tunnel, then let commuters respond over multiple days.
What is actually modeled?

Driving is microscopic, not a congestion formula. Every driving commuter becomes a vehicle on a directed street network. Cars have positions and speeds, maintain a minimum following gap, stop at red lights, wait if the next street is full, and can create queues that spill backward across the network. Side-street background traffic is generated independently and competes for green time at three signalized intersections.

The old commuter route follows the arterial around the mountain. The tunnel creates a shorter edge through it. There is no equation that says “N drivers means X minutes”; a driver's trip time is simply arrival time minus departure time. The displayed driving average is the mean of those measured trips.

Transit riders are also individual commuters. Each walks to the station, arrives at a particular time, catches the next scheduled train, rides downtown, and walks to the destination. Service headway is adjusted between mornings based on total ridership, providing the feedback needed for a Downs-Thomson mechanism: losing riders makes transit less convenient.

After each morning, commuters compare the observed average times. A small number whose current choice is sufficiently worse than the alternative change modes for the next day, subject to individual switching reluctance. Stable means nobody currently has enough incentive to change modes; it is an agent-based approximation to a mode-choice equilibrium, not an algebraically imposed answer.
