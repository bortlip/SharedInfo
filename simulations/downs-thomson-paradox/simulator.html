The mechanism

1Tunnel shortens the physical driving route by five free-flow minutes.
2Transit riders observe the faster driving day and some switch modes.
3More individual cars hit the same downtown bottleneck, so a visible queue grows.
4Fewer transit riders justify less frequent service, increasing waits at the station.
5The system settles only when neither mode offers a meaningful advantage.
The paradox has not happened yet. That is what the tunnel button is for.
What is actually modeled?

People: 100 persistent commuter agents, each with a fixed morning departure offset and a current travel mode. At most two commuters switch after a day when the observed average times differ by more than 0.25 minutes. Limiting daily switching makes adaptation visible instead of teleporting directly to an equilibrium.

Road: every driver creates one car. Cars move at the same free-flow speed, maintain spacing behind the car ahead, and encounter a downtown bottleneck that can discharge only one car every 0.85 simulated minutes. When arrivals exceed that capacity they stop behind one another, producing the queue and measured travel delay. The mountain route requires 34 free-flow minutes; the tunnel route requires 29.

Transit: riders walk six minutes to a station, catch the next scheduled train, ride for 28 minutes, and walk 7.1 minutes at the downtown end. Train headway responds to ridership, ranging from roughly six minutes with strong demand to roughly twenty-two with weak demand. Each rider's wait is determined by their station arrival and the actual timetable.

Equilibrium: this is an agent-based approximation rather than an algebraically imposed Nash solution. The city is called stable when the two observed mode averages are close enough that nobody is scheduled to switch the following day. The numerical result depends on the explicit road capacity, departure pattern, service rule, and switching rule; that dependence is a feature, not a rounding error wearing a fake mustache.
