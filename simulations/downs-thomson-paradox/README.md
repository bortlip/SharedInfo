# Downs-Thomson Traffic Lab

An agent-based, dependency-free browser simulation of the Downs-Thomson paradox. One hundred persistent commuters choose between driving and transit; every driver becomes an individual car that moves through the road network and queues behind other cars, while transit riders catch scheduled trains whose service frequency responds to ridership.

- [Open the released simulation](https://bortlip.github.io/SharedInfo/simulations/downs-thomson-paradox/)

## What is simulated

### Commuters

There are 100 persistent people. Each has a fixed morning departure offset and a current mode. The default city starts near an established pre-tunnel split of 37 drivers and 63 transit riders. After each completed morning, the measured average times are compared. If one mode is more than 0.25 minutes faster, at most two commuters from the slower mode switch for the following day. The small daily adjustment makes the approach to equilibrium visible rather than jumping directly to an algebraic solution.

### Road traffic

There is no `base + congestion * drivers` travel-time formula.

Each driver creates one car. Cars:

- enter the road at their commuter's departure time;
- move at a common free-flow speed;
- maintain spacing behind the car ahead;
- follow the mountain loop before the tunnel or the shorter tunnel route afterward;
- pass through a finite-capacity downtown bottleneck that can discharge one car every 0.85 simulated minutes;
- stop behind one another when arrivals exceed that discharge capacity.

The mountain route takes 34 minutes at free flow. The tunnel route takes 29 minutes at free flow. Actual driving time is measured from each car's departure until it reaches downtown, so congestion delay comes from the queue generated by individual vehicles.

### Transit

Transit riders walk six minutes to the station, wait for the next scheduled train, ride for 28 minutes, and walk 7.1 minutes at the downtown end. Service headway responds to current ridership, from roughly six minutes under strong demand to roughly twenty-two under weak demand. Each person's wait depends on their own station-arrival time and the timetable.

This feedback is the part of the Downs-Thomson mechanism that a road-only traffic simulation would miss: when a road improvement attracts riders into cars, transit can become less frequent or otherwise less attractive.

## What to try

1. Run the baseline morning. Around the initial 37/63 split, measured driving and transit times should be close enough that few or no people switch.
2. Build the tunnel and run another morning without changing anyone's mode first. Drivers get the physical five-minute shortcut immediately.
3. Use **Run until stable**. A few transit riders switch to driving each day. More individual cars build a longer queue at the unchanged downtown bottleneck, while falling transit ridership stretches the train headway.
4. The post-tunnel city stabilizes only after the two experienced mode times become similar again. With the default deterministic population and parameters, that stable commute is worse than the pre-tunnel one even though the road itself remains five free-flow minutes shorter.

This is a teaching microsimulation, not a calibrated transportation forecast. Its point is to make the causal machinery visible: physical capacity, queues, schedules, individual trips, repeated mode choice, and feedback between road and transit usage.
